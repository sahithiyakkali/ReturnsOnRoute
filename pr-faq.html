Returns on Route: PR/FAQ

The concept written in Amazon’s PR/FAQ format: a press release first, then the customer and internal questions it has to survive.

An independent concept written as a product exercise. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Amazon. All company statements and details below are fictional and illustrative.

Part 1: Press release

Amazon Introduces Returns on Route: Return Items to the Delivery Driver or a Campus Kiosk, No Trip Required

Customers in apartment buildings and on college campuses can now hand off bulky returns in under a minute

SEATTLE — Amazon today announced Returns on Route, a new way for customers without easy access to a return location to send items back. Customers who live in participating buildings can hand an eligible return to the Amazon delivery driver already visiting their address. On campuses and in college towns, customers can use a secure self-serve return kiosk.

For many customers without a car, the closest return point can be a 15-minute walk or more. For heavy or bulky items, that effort can be enough to stop people from buying in the first place. Returns on Route removes the trip.

With Returns on Route, customers start a return in the Amazon app and receive a QR code, with no label to print. When a delivery driver is about 10 minutes from the customer’s building, the app sends an alert that the return window is open and shows whether the van has space. The customer walks to the van, the driver scans the code, and the item is loaded in about 30 seconds. Customers receive their refund confirmation right away for eligible items.

On campuses, customers scan the same QR code at a secure kiosk, drop the item in an opened compartment, and receive a confirmation. The kiosk is emptied during regular delivery stops.

How it works

  1. Start a return in the Amazon app and select an eligible item.
  2. Get a QR code.
  3. Receive an alert when your driver is nearby and the van has space.
  4. Hand the item to the driver, who scans the code. Or scan it at a campus kiosk.
  5. Get your refund confirmation.

Availability

Returns on Route launches as a pilot at select student housing and campus locations for bulky items such as cookware, small furniture, electronics, home decor, and larger beauty products. Customers can check eligibility in the Amazon app under Returns.

Part 2: Customer FAQ

Who is Returns on Route for?

Customers who live in participating apartment buildings or campuses and don’t have easy access to a return location, especially those without a car.

What can I return this way?

During the pilot, bulky or heavy items (roughly over 5 lbs or 12 inches) in categories like cookware, small furniture, electronics, home decor, and larger beauty products. Items must otherwise be eligible for return under Amazon’s normal policy.

Do I need to print a label or repackage anything?

No label is needed. Items should be in a condition that can be safely handled and loaded.

What if the van is full?

The app shows van capacity before you walk down. If there’s no space, you’ll see the next available window or can use another return method.

What if I miss the driver?

The return isn’t lost. Your QR code stays valid for a set period, and you can try the next delivery or use another return option.

When do I get my refund?

For lower-risk items, your refund starts when the driver scans the item. For higher-value items, it starts when the item is scanned at the warehouse.

Is it safe to walk up to a delivery van?

The app shows a designated meeting spot and instructions. Customers should only approach when the van is safely parked.

Does it cost anything?

No extra charge during the pilot.

What about perishable items?

Not included. Spoiled or damaged perishables are handled through the existing refund process.

Part 3: Internal FAQ

Customer and market

What problem are we solving?

Car-free customers in dense housing often live far from a return point. For bulky items, the effort of returning leads them to avoid buying, and our current workarounds (carrier pickups that require scheduling, and “keep it” refunds) are costly or frustrating.

What evidence supports this?

A set of informal interviews with 32 residents of one student apartment building. 24 had no car, and all 24 said return effort makes them hesitate to order. None of the 8 residents with a car reported this. All 24 car-free residents wanted an easy on-site drop-off, and 20 of 24 liked handing returns to drivers during normal deliveries. Hesitation began around 5 to 10 lbs and 12 to 18 inches.

Limits: one building, mostly students, self-reported, no dollar sizing. A larger structured study and order data analysis would be required before launch.

How big is the opportunity?

Not yet sized. It would require estimating the number of car-free customers in dense housing, the value of skipped orders in bulky categories, and the current cost of carrier pickups and keep-it refunds. Pulling public return-volume and carrier-cost data is the next step in the case study.

Why not just add more drop-off locations?

New locations don’t reach customers who still have to carry bulky items on foot. Returns on Route goes to the customer instead.

Operations

How much time does this add to a driver’s stop?

The target is under 30 seconds per handoff. This is the central risk and the first thing the pilot measures.

How do we protect driver performance and pay?

Handoffs are capped per stop, vans show capacity in advance, and drivers receive a per-return incentive. If time targets can’t be met, the pilot stops.

What if vans fill up?

Capacity is displayed in the app. Returns can be offered in later windows when vans are emptier, and customers fall back to existing return methods.

How are returned items transported to the warehouse?

Items are scanned at handoff, carried in a designated return area of the van, and scanned again at the station or warehouse.

How do campus kiosks work?

A secure unit opens via QR code. A driver or logistics partner empties it at a regular stop, with scans at each step. Hosts such as campuses or property managers provide space.

Risk and trust

What about fraud?

QR codes are single-use and tied to the account and item. Lower-risk items refund at handoff, similar to the scan-and-go returns customers already see at stores. Higher-value items refund after warehouse verification. Fraud rates are tracked against the standard return flow.

Who is liable for lost or damaged returns?

To be defined with Legal and delivery partners. The pilot starts with lower-value items and full scan tracking to limit exposure.

What about customer and driver safety?

Designated meeting spots, clear instructions, and building-approved locations. Safety incidents are a kill criterion.

Business

What does success look like?

Adoption of Returns on Route among eligible returns, shorter return time, increased orders in bulky categories at pilot sites versus comparison sites, and lower cost per return than carrier pickups and keep-it refunds.

What would make us stop?

Added time per stop stays above target, cost per return doesn’t beat current alternatives without a sales lift, or fraud and loss rates are materially higher than standard returns.

What are we not doing?

Perishables, suburban or car-owning customers, and international markets.

What are the biggest open questions?

  1. Can driver handoffs stay under 30 seconds in practice?
  2. Does easier returning measurably increase bulky-item purchases?
  3. Is the unit cost lower than current alternatives?
  4. How large is the car-free dense-housing segment nationally?
